John 1:1-3 — The Eternal Word and Creation

A closer look at John's opening words, the Greek terms he uses, the relationship between the Word and God, and what these verses reveal about Jesus Christ before creation.

Why John 1:1-3 Matters

John begins his Gospel before Bethlehem, before Abraham, and even before creation. These opening verses answer a foundational question: Who is Jesus Christ? John presents the Word as already existing in the beginning, personally related to God, truly divine, and involved in the coming into existence of everything that was made.

Central idea: John does not introduce Jesus merely as a teacher who appeared in history. He introduces Him as the eternal Word through whom creation came into being.

John 1:1 — “In the beginning was the Word”

Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος
En archē ēn ho logos
Approximate pronunciation: “en ar-KHAY ane ho LO-gos”

“In the beginning”

John's opening deliberately echoes Genesis 1:1. Genesis begins with the creation of the heavens and the earth. John reaches back to that same beginning and tells us that the Word was already there.

The importance of ēn — “was”

The Greek word ἦν (ēn) is an imperfect form of the verb “to be.” In this context it describes the Word as already existing when “the beginning” is in view. John does not say that the Word came into existence in the beginning. He says the Word was.

Important: The theology does not rest on the verb tense by itself. The conclusion comes from the whole sentence and from John's contrast between the Word who “was” and created things that later “came into being.”

“The Word” — Logos

λόγος (logos) can mean word, message, expression, or reason, depending on context. John later identifies this Word as the One who became flesh (John 1:14), and the Gospel makes clear that he is speaking of Jesus Christ.

John 1:1 — “The Word was with God”

καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν
kai ho logos ēn pros ton theon

John now distinguishes the Word from the One He is “with.” The Word is not simply another name for the Father. There is personal relationship and distinction.

The preposition πρός (pros) can express movement toward or relationship with someone. Here it supports the idea of personal fellowship between the Word and God. John is preparing us to understand both distinction and unity.

What John is saying: The Word is personally distinguishable from God the Father, yet He is not outside the divine identity.

John 1:1 — “The Word was God”

καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος
kai theos ēn ho logos

John has just said that the Word was “with God,” and now he says that the Word “was God.” He is not saying that the Word is the same person as the Father. Instead, he is describing the Word as sharing the divine nature while remaining personally distinct.

Why does theos not have “the” in front of it?

Greek does not use the definite article in exactly the same way English does. The absence of an article before theos does not automatically make the phrase mean “a god.” In this word order, theos functions as the predicate describing what the Word is by nature. The grammar must be read together with the context.

Careful conclusion: John 1:1 presents the Word as personally distinct from the Father and truly divine. It should not be reduced either to “the Word is the Father” or to “the Word is merely a lesser created god.”

John 1:2 — “He was in the beginning with God”

Verse 2 repeats and gathers the truths of verse 1 before John turns to creation. The Word was already present in the beginning, and He was already in relationship with God. This prepares the reader for verse 3: the Word does not belong to the category of things that came into existence.

Later in John's Gospel, Jesus speaks of the glory He had with the Father before the world existed (John 17:5), which fits naturally with the opening statement here.

John 1:3 — “All things came into being through Him”

πάντα δι' αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο
panta di' autou egeneto
Approximate pronunciation of egeneto: “eh-GEH-neh-toh”

The importance of egeneto

The Greek word ἐγένετο (egeneto) comes from ginomai and means “came to be,” “became,” or “came into existence,” depending on context. John uses this word when speaking about created things.

The Word Creation
ēn — “was” egeneto — “came into being”
Already existing in the beginning Things that came into existence

John then strengthens the statement by saying that apart from the Word, not one thing that has come into being came into being. The point is sweeping: everything in the created order owes its existence to God's creative work through the Word.

The major theological implication: If everything that came into existence came into existence through the Word, then the Word is not presented as one of the created things. John places Him on the Creator side of the Creator-creation distinction.

How the Three Verses Work Together

Verse 1 — The Word is eternal, personally distinct from the Father, and divine.

Verse 2 — The Word already existed in relationship with God in the beginning.

Verse 3 — Everything that came into existence came into existence through the Word.

John is preparing the reader for the astonishing statement in John 1:14: the eternal Word entered human history and became flesh. The Creator entered His creation.

Helpful Cross-References

  • Genesis 1:1 — the beginning of creation.
  • John 1:14 — the Word became flesh.
  • John 17:5 — Jesus speaks of glory with the Father before the world existed.
  • Colossians 1:16-17 — creation is described in relation to Christ.
  • Hebrews 1:2-3 — God made the ages through the Son.

Questions for Deeper Study

  1. Why do you think John begins his Gospel with Genesis-like language?
  2. What is the difference between saying the Word “was” and creation “came into being”?
  3. How can the Word be “with God” and also “be God”?
  4. What does verse 3 imply about the claim that the Word was created?
  5. How does John 1:14 change the way we understand verses 1-3?

HBM In-Depth Study Principle
